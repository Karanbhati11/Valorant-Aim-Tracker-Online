What Counts as Low Sens vs High Sens?

Before arguing about which is better, you need a shared language. Raw in-game sensitivity numbers are meaningless on their own because they depend on your mouse DPI and the game's internal scaling. Two players both running "2.0 sensitivity" can have wildly different on-screen speeds. That is why serious players measure sensitivity two better ways: cm/360 and eDPI.

cm/360: The Most Honest Measure

cm/360 is the number of centimeters you physically move your mouse to complete a full 360-degree turn in game. It ignores DPI math entirely and describes real hand movement, which is what actually trains your muscle memory. As a rough framework:

  • Low sensitivity: around 35 cm/360 or higher. You move your whole arm to turn, and a full spin can sweep most of a large mousepad.
  • Medium sensitivity: roughly 25 to 35 cm/360. A balance most players land in.
  • High sensitivity: under about 25 cm/360. A small wrist flick spins you around quickly.

These bands are guidelines, not laws. Different communities draw the lines in slightly different places, but the spirit holds: more centimeters per turn equals lower sensitivity.

eDPI: Comparing Across Games

eDPI (effective DPI) is your mouse DPI multiplied by your in-game sensitivity. It lets you compare your speed against other players in the same game on a single number, and it lets you keep a consistent feel when you swap titles. Low Sens Benefits

The main case for low sensitivity is precision and repeatability. When a full turn takes 40-plus centimeters, every degree of aim is spread across more physical distance, so the margin for error on any single shot is larger. That makes consistent flicks and clean micro-adjustments easier to land. Low sens also tends to reward deliberate, controlled play, which is why it is so common in tactical shooters where one accurate shot decides a duel. The cost is obvious: you need room on your desk, and snapping to a target behind you takes a real arm movement rather than a flick.

High Sens Benefits

High sensitivity buys you speed and economy of motion. You can whip around to check your back, reposition during a movement-heavy fight, or stay glued to a target juking at close range without lifting and repositioning your mouse. It works beautifully in small spaces and for players who favor reactive, fast-paced gameplay. The tradeoff is that precision becomes harder. A nervous hand or a tiny tremor turns into a missed shot, and recoil control or long-range tapping can feel jittery until you build the fine motor control to tame it.

Arm Aiming vs Wrist Aiming

The low sens vs high sens question is really a question about which joint drives your aim. This is the heart of the arm aiming vs wrist aiming discussion, and it explains why the same setting feels great for one player and awful for another.

  • Arm aiming uses the elbow and shoulder as the pivot for large movements, with the wrist handling fine corrections. It pairs naturally with low sensitivity because you have the physical distance to make big motions accurate. Arm aimers tend to be very consistent but need space and a stable posture.
  • Wrist aiming drives most movement from the wrist and fingers. It pairs with higher sensitivity, since a small flick covers a lot of screen. Wrist aiming is fast and comfortable in tight setups but can lose precision on long, sweeping motions.

Most strong players actually use a hybrid: the arm handles large repositioning while the wrist and fingers handle micro-adjustment. Rather than forcing yourself into one camp, notice which joint you instinctively reach for and choose a sensitivity that supports it. If you naturally plant your wrist and flick from it, an ultra-low setting will fight you. If you anchor your elbow and sweep, a very high setting will feel uncontrollable.

The "It Depends" Truth and How to Actually Pick

Here is the part nobody likes to hear: there is no single best sensitivity for aiming that applies to everyone. The right number depends on your hand size, grip, desk space, the game, and your personal aiming style. That sounds like a dodge, but it is actually empowering, because it means you can stop chasing a magic number and start running a simple, repeatable process to find yours.

  1. Start in the genre's range. Use the table above to pick a sensible starting cm/360 for your main game rather than guessing.
  2. Set a stable DPI. Pick one DPI (800 is a common, reliable choice) and tune in-game sensitivity from there instead of constantly changing both.
  3. Check the full-turn test. You should be able to do a comfortable 180 without lifting your mouse, while still being able to hold steady on a small distant target. If either feels impossible, adjust toward the middle.
  4. Commit before judging. Give any new setting at least one to two weeks. Muscle memory needs reps to form, and a setting always feels wrong for the first few days.
  5. Adjust in small steps. Change by 10 percent at most, never double or halve. Big jumps reset your muscle memory and tell you nothing useful.

How to Test Both Fairly

Most "I tried low sens and hated it" stories are really stories of an unfair test. Switching to a radically different setting and jumping straight into ranked guarantees frustration. To compare low and high sensitivity honestly, control the variables and measure something concrete.

  • Test in a trainer, not ranked. Use the same drills for each setting so the only thing changing is sensitivity. A controlled environment removes teammates, map luck, and tilt from the equation.
  • Run identical sessions. Pick a fixed set of flicking, tracking, and micro-adjustment exercises, and record your scores for low, medium, and high over several days.
  • Warm up first. Cold scores are noisy. Warm up before logging any numbers you plan to compare.
  • Track trends, not single runs. One great or terrible session means nothing. Look at the average across many runs to see which range you are genuinely more accurate and consistent on.
  • Keep everything else fixed. Same DPI, same mousepad, same posture, same crosshair. Change one variable at a time or you learn nothing.

Why Consistency Beats the Perfect Number

Here is the single most important idea in this entire debate: the player who commits to a reasonable sensitivity and trains it for months will out-aim the player who switches every week chasing perfection. Your brain builds aim as a closed-loop motor skill. Every time you change sensitivity meaningfully, you partially reset that skill and start relearning the relationship between hand movement and screen movement.

This is why obsessing over whether you should be at 34 or 36 cm/360 is mostly wasted energy. The difference between two nearby settings is tiny compared to the difference between a trained setting and an untrained one. Pick something sane for your genre and style, confirm it passes the full-turn test, then stop tinkering and start grinding reps. Consistency is the real cheat code, and no perfect number can substitute for the muscle memory you build by sticking with one.

Both low and high sensitivity have produced world-class aimers. That alone proves the setting is a tool, not a destiny. Choose the tool that fits your hand and your game, then put in the focused practice that actually moves the needle.

Frequently Asked Questions

Do pros use low or high sensitivity?

It varies heavily by game. In tactical shooters like Valorant and CS2, most pros lean toward lower sensitivities for precision and consistent taps, often in the 30 to 50 cm/360 range. In faster, movement-heavy or arena games, you will find more medium-to-high settings. Even within a single game there is a spread, which is the clearest proof that the right answer depends on the player, not a universal rule.

Is low sensitivity better for accuracy?

For many players, yes, low sensitivity makes precise micro-adjustment and consistent flicks easier because each degree of aim is spread across more physical mouse movement, so small errors are simpler to correct. But it is not automatically better. If you lack desk space or naturally aim from the wrist, a sensitivity that is too low can actually hurt your accuracy by forcing awkward, repositioned motions. Accuracy comes from a setting that matches your style plus consistent training, not from low numbers alone.

How do I switch to a lower sensitivity?

Switch gradually rather than all at once. Drop your sensitivity by about 10 percent at a time, give each step a week or two of practice in an aim trainer and live play, then drop again once it feels natural. A sudden large change feels broken and tempts you to quit before muscle memory adapts. Keep your DPI fixed and only change the in-game value so the transition stays predictable, and make sure you have enough mousepad space for the larger arm movements.

What is a good cm/360 to start with?

A balanced starting point for most players is somewhere around 25 to 35 cm/360. From there, lean lower if you play precision-focused tactical shooters and want maximum stability, or lean higher if you play fast movement games and need quick turns. Treat the starting value as a region to explore, confirm it passes the full-turn test, then refine in small steps based on your trainer scores.

Does sensitivity matter more than DPI?

What actually matters is the combination, best understood as your cm/360 or eDPI. DPI and in-game sensitivity multiply together to produce your real on-screen speed, so neither number means much in isolation. The practical advice is to lock in one sensible DPI and adjust only the in-game value, which keeps your effective sensitivity consistent and easy to reason about across sessions and games.
