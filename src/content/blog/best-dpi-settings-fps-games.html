What DPI Actually Is

DPI stands for "dots per inch," and it describes how many counts your mouse sensor reports for every inch you physically move the device across your pad. A higher DPI means more reported counts per inch, so your cursor or in-game view travels farther for the same physical hand motion. A lower DPI reports fewer counts per inch, so the same hand motion moves the view less. Many manufacturers also use the term CPI (counts per inch), which is technically more accurate, but the two words are used interchangeably in gaming.

The key thing to understand is that DPI on its own does not define how fast your aim feels in a game. It is only one half of the equation. The actual on-screen speed you experience is the product of your DPI and your in-game sensitivity slider working together. This is why two players can both run the same DPI yet have wildly different aim, and why copying someone's DPI without copying their sensitivity tells you almost nothing.

DPI vs Sensitivity: Understanding eDPI

The relationship between DPI vs sensitivity is the single most misunderstood topic in aim setup. People obsess over the DPI number printed on their mouse box while ignoring that the in-game sensitivity multiplier matters just as much. The way to combine both into one meaningful number is called eDPI, or "effective DPI."

The formula is simple:

  • eDPI = mouse DPI × in-game sensitivity

eDPI represents your true, on-screen turning speed regardless of how you split the two values. A player on 1600 DPI with 0.25 sensitivity has the exact same effective aim speed as a player on 800 DPI with 0.5 sensitivity, because both produce an eDPI of 400. This is the only fair way to compare aim speed between players or even between your own past settings. When someone shares their config, the eDPI tells you far more than the raw DPI ever could.

Below are a few examples showing how different DPI and sensitivity combinations land on the same effective speed.

400 1.0 400 Low / precise
800 0.5 400 Low / precise (identical to above)
800 1.0 800 Medium
1600 0.5 800 Medium (identical to above)
800 2.0 1600 High / fast

Notice that the 400-eDPI rows feel identical even though one uses 400 DPI and the other uses 800 DPI. That equivalence is the heart of why the specific DPI number matters less than people think, and why you should treat DPI as a fixed base rather than a dial you constantly twist. The Best DPI Settings for FPS Games: 400 vs 800

If you want the short answer, the best gaming DPI for the vast majority of FPS players is either 400 DPI or 800 DPI. These two values dominate competitive play for several practical reasons, and you rarely need to look beyond them.

Why 800 DPI Is the Modern Default

For years, 800 DPI has been the most common recommendation for new and experienced players alike. It sits in the sweet spot for modern sensors and high-refresh monitors. At 800 DPI you get smooth, granular cursor movement without forcing your in-game sensitivity slider down to extreme values that can feel mushy. It also pairs cleanly with 1440p and higher resolutions, where slightly more reported counts help your aim feel responsive across more pixels.

Why 400 DPI Still Has a Following

Plenty of professionals, especially long-time Counter-Strike players, still swear by 400 DPI. The reasoning is historical and practical: older sensors were optimized and tested heavily around 400, and many veterans simply never had a reason to change. At 400 DPI you typically run a higher in-game sensitivity value to reach the same eDPI, which some players find gives them a clearer mental model of their settings. For most people today, though, 400 and 800 are functionally equivalent once eDPI is matched.

What DPI Do Pros Use?

If you are wondering what DPI do pros use, the honest answer is that the large majority cluster at 400 or 800 DPI and then tune sensitivity to hit a comfortable eDPI. Tactical shooter pros tend to run lower effective speeds for precise tap-firing, while pros in faster, movement-heavy titles run higher effective speeds for quick turns. The DPI itself is almost never an exotic number. You will basically never see a serious competitor playing on 6400 DPI. That alone should tell you that chasing a high DPI is the wrong goal.

The Myth That Higher DPI Equals Better Aim

Marketing has convinced a lot of players that a mouse advertising 20,000 or 30,000 DPI will somehow make them aim better. It will not. A massive DPI number is a spec-sheet bragging point, not a performance advantage for aiming. Here is why higher DPI does not improve your aim:

  • You compensate with lower sensitivity anyway. If you raise your DPI, you have to drop your in-game sensitivity to keep the same eDPI. The net feel is identical, so you gained nothing.
  • Extremely high DPI can introduce jitter. At very high counts, tiny hand tremors and sensor noise become more visible, which can make precise micro-adjustments harder rather than easier.
  • It does not add information. Past a certain point, more counts per inch do not give you finer control than your monitor's pixel grid can actually display at normal sensitivities.

The real takeaway is that mouse DPI for gaming should be set high enough to be smooth and low enough to stay stable, then left alone. For nearly everyone, that means 400 or 800, full stop.

Sensor Considerations and Native DPI Steps

Modern gaming sensors are excellent, but they still have characteristics worth knowing. Most quality sensors have "native" DPI steps, which are the values the sensor is designed to report most cleanly. Common native steps include 400, 800, 1600, and 3200. Choosing a native step rather than an arbitrary value like 750 or 1100 ensures your sensor is not interpolating, which keeps tracking accurate and avoids subtle inconsistencies.

A few practical guidelines:

  • Stick to clean, native steps. 400 and 800 are native on essentially every gaming mouse made in the last decade.
  • Avoid software-interpolated DPI values that fall between native steps. They can introduce minor inaccuracy without any benefit.
  • Trust modern sensors. The flagship sensors in current mice are accurate enough that DPI choice within the native range is about feel and habit, not raw tracking quality.

Polling Rate: The Other Setting That Matters

While you are configuring your mouse, set your polling rate correctly too. Polling rate, measured in hertz, is how often your mouse reports its position to the computer each second. A 1000 Hz polling rate reports 1000 times per second, or once every millisecond, and has been the competitive standard for years. Some newer mice offer 4000 Hz or 8000 Hz, which can reduce latency further but demand more from your CPU and offer diminishing returns for most players.

For the vast majority of setups, 1000 Hz is the right choice: it is smooth, low-latency, and stable on virtually any system. Only consider higher polling rates if you have a powerful CPU and a high-refresh monitor, and even then, test that it does not cause stutters or frame drops. Polling rate and DPI are independent settings, so configure both deliberately rather than assuming a single "gaming mode" preset handles everything.

Set DPI Once, Then Tune Sensitivity

The most important workflow lesson in this entire guide is this: set your DPI once and never touch it again. Use your in-game sensitivity slider for all future adjustments. There are concrete reasons this approach wins.

  1. Consistency across games. A fixed DPI gives you a stable base. You can then match eDPI across Valorant, CS2, Apex, and Fortnite so your muscle memory transfers instead of resetting every time you switch titles.
  2. Finer control. In-game sensitivity sliders usually offer far more granular adjustment than DPI steps, letting you dial in exactly the feel you want.
  3. Easier troubleshooting. When your aim feels off, you only have one variable to think about. A moving DPI plus a moving sensitivity is a recipe for never finding a setting that sticks.

Pick 800 DPI if you are unsure, leave it there permanently, and make every future change through sensitivity. If you are still deciding how fast that sensitivity should be, our comparison of low vs high sensitivity in FPS games walks through the tradeoffs in detail.

How to Test Your DPI in an Aim Trainer

Numbers on a config screen mean nothing until your hand confirms them. The fastest way to validate a DPI and sensitivity combination is to test it deliberately in an aim trainer before you take it into ranked. A browser-based 3D trainer lets you isolate mechanics and feel small changes immediately.

Here is a simple testing routine:

  • Check your 180 turn. Measure how much physical desk space a full turn takes. You want to be able to flick 180 degrees without lifting the mouse, while still landing precise small adjustments.
  • Run a flicking scenario. Spawn static targets and snap to them. If you consistently overshoot, your effective speed is too high; if you constantly fall short, it is too low.
  • Run a tracking scenario. Follow a moving target and watch whether your crosshair stays glued or wobbles. Jittery tracking can indicate your eDPI is too fast for fine control.
  • Test target switching. Move between multiple targets to confirm your setting holds up when you need both speed and precision in the same motion.

Give any new setting at least a few sessions before judging it. Your brain needs reps to adapt, and first impressions are unreliable. Pairing this with a clean, high-contrast crosshair makes it easier to see exactly where your shots land while you tune. Frequently Asked Questions

Is 800 DPI good for FPS games?

Yes. 800 DPI is the most widely recommended setting for FPS games and works well on modern sensors and high-resolution monitors. It is smooth, stable, and pairs cleanly with a wide range of in-game sensitivities. If you have no specific reason to choose otherwise, 800 DPI is the safest starting point.

Does higher DPI improve aim?

No. Higher DPI does not improve aim because you simply lower your in-game sensitivity to compensate, leaving the actual on-screen speed unchanged. Very high DPI can even amplify sensor noise and hand tremor, making precise micro-adjustments harder. What matters is your effective DPI, not the raw number on the box.

What DPI do most pros use?

Most professional players use either 400 or 800 DPI and then adjust their in-game sensitivity to reach a comfortable eDPI. Exotic high-DPI values are essentially never used at the top level. The clustering around these two values is strong evidence that a moderate, stable DPI beats a high one.

Should I use 400 or 800 DPI?

Both are excellent, and once you match eDPI they feel nearly identical. 800 DPI is the modern default and tends to feel a touch smoother on high-resolution displays, while 400 DPI remains popular among veteran Counter-Strike players. Pick one, match your eDPI, and commit rather than bouncing between them.

What is the difference between DPI and eDPI?

DPI is the raw sensitivity of your mouse sensor, while eDPI is your DPI multiplied by your in-game sensitivity, representing your true on-screen turning speed. eDPI is the better number for comparing settings between players because it accounts for both halves of the equation rather than just the hardware.
