Why Sensitivity Matters More Than You Think

Sensitivity is the bridge between your physical hand movement and what happens on screen. Set it too high and tiny twitches send your crosshair flying past the target, forcing constant overcorrection. Set it too low and you run out of mousepad mid-flick or feel sluggish reacting to fast peeks. The right best sensitivity for aiming is the one that lets you do a full turn comfortably while still nailing the small micro-adjustments that win duels. Most players never find this balance because they change settings every few days, chasing a feeling instead of building a habit.

Here is the core idea to internalize before anything else: consistency beats the exact number. A player who keeps one steady setting for months will out-aim a player with a theoretically "better" value who resets it every week. Your brain is constantly calibrating how far to move your hand for a given on-screen distance. Every time you change mouse sensitivity FPS settings, you reset that calibration and start the learning curve over.

Understanding DPI and Sensitivity

To talk about sensitivity precisely, you need two numbers working together.

What DPI Is

DPI (dots per inch), sometimes called CPI, is a hardware setting on your mouse. It controls how many counts the sensor reports for each inch you physically move the mouse. Higher DPI means the cursor travels farther on screen for the same hand motion. DPI lives in your mouse software or onboard memory, independent of any game.

What In-Game Sensitivity Is

In-game sensitivity is a multiplier the game applies on top of your DPI. Each title uses its own scale, which is exactly why raw sensitivity numbers do not transfer between games. A sensitivity of 2.0 in one shooter can feel completely different from 2.0 in another because the underlying math differs.

What eDPI Is

This is where eDPI solves the confusion. eDPI stands for effective dots per inch, and the formula is simple:

eDPI = DPI × in-game sensitivity

Because it combines both values into one number, eDPI describes your true on-screen speed regardless of how you split it between hardware and software. For example, 800 DPI at 4.0 sensitivity gives an eDPI of 3200, and so does 1600 DPI at 2.0. Treating eDPI as a manual eDPI calculator lets you compare yourself fairly to other players and, more importantly, keep a consistent feel when you move between titles. When people talk about DPI and sensitivity as a single concept, eDPI is the number they should really be comparing.

cm/360: The Most Honest Measure of Feel

eDPI is great for comparison, but it still depends on monitor and game scaling. The most universal way to describe sensitivity is cm per 360, often written cm/360. This is the physical distance, in centimeters, that you move your mouse to spin a full 360 degrees in game. It answers the only question that truly matters to your muscle memory: how far does my hand travel to turn all the way around?

cm/360 is powerful because it is measured in the real world, not in arbitrary game units. A 30 cm/360 turn feels the same amount of physical effort no matter which shooter you are in, even if the eDPI numbers look different on paper. Lower cm/360 (like 15 cm) means a fast, twitchy setup where small movements turn you a long way. Higher cm/360 (like 45 cm) means a slow, deliberate setup that rewards arm aiming and precision. Most tactical FPS players land somewhere between 25 and 45 cm/360.

Arm Aiming vs Wrist Aiming

Your sensitivity choice and your aiming style are deeply linked, so it helps to understand the two main approaches.

  • Arm aiming uses your elbow and shoulder as the pivot for large movements, with the wrist handling fine adjustments. It pairs naturally with lower sensitivity (higher cm/360) because you have the physical space to make big, stable motions. Arm aiming is generally more consistent and less prone to fatigue-driven shakiness, which is why many precision-focused players prefer it.
  • Wrist aiming keeps the forearm planted and drives most of the motion from the wrist. It works well with higher sensitivity (lower cm/360) because small wrist flicks cover the screen. It can feel faster, but it is harder to keep consistent over long sessions and offers less room for micro-correction.

Most strong players actually use a hybrid: arm for big repositioning flicks and large turns, wrist for tiny corrections. If your sensitivity is so high that you never use your arm, consider lowering it. A setting that forces you to engage your arm for normal aiming tends to produce steadier, more repeatable results.

How to Find Your Perfect Sensitivity: A Step-by-Step Method

This is the practical part. Follow these steps to find your sensitivity and converge on a value instead of guessing forever. Give the process a few sessions rather than rushing it in five minutes.

  1. Lock your DPI first. Set your mouse to a stable, common DPI such as 800 and leave it there. From now on you will tune feel using in-game sensitivity, not by changing DPI. This keeps every comparison clean.
  2. Pick a sensible starting point. If you have no reference, aim for roughly 30–40 cm/360, which lands most players in a controllable zone. Convert that to your game's sensitivity value or just start near a medium eDPI like 400 and adjust from there.
  3. Measure your cm/360. Mark a start point on your mousepad, do a clean in-game 360, and measure how far the mouse traveled. Write the number down. This becomes your anchor across every game.
  4. Test flicking. In an aim trainer, run a flick or click-timing drill. If you constantly overshoot targets, your sensitivity is too high. If you arrive late and feel slow, it is too low.
  5. Test tracking and micro-adjustment. Run a tracking drill on moving targets. Watch whether you can hold the crosshair steady. Jittery, twitchy tracking points to a setting that is too fast.
  6. Adjust in small steps. Change your value by only about 10 percent at a time. Big jumps make it impossible to feel what improved. Re-measure your cm/360 after each change so you always know where you are.
  7. Commit for two weeks. Once a value feels close, stop tweaking and play with it for at least two weeks. Muscle memory needs repetition to form. Resist the urge to change after a single bad session.
  8. Re-evaluate, then lock it. After the commitment period, judge by your overall consistency, not your best clip. If you are landing shots reliably and rarely overshooting, you have found your number. Lock it and stop searching.

While dialing this in, do not overlook your crosshair. A clean, high-contrast reticle helps your eyes lock onto targets faster, which makes it much easier to judge whether a sensitivity change is genuinely helping or just feels different.

Why Consistency Beats the Exact Number

It bears repeating because it is the most common mistake: the specific value matters far less than your willingness to stick with it. Aim is a closed-loop motor skill. Your brain learns, through thousands of reps, exactly how far to move your hand for a given on-screen distance. That learning only compounds if the input stays the same. Every settings change wipes part of that progress.

Two players with identical hardware can have very different "perfect" sensitivities, and both can be elite, simply because each committed and built deep muscle memory. The players who never improve are usually the ones endlessly copying the latest pro config. Pick a reasonable value from the ranges above, then protect it. Treat changing your sensitivity like a serious decision, not a daily experiment.

How to Keep the Same eDPI Across Games

Once you find a value you love, you will want the same feel in every shooter you play. Because each game scales sensitivity differently, you cannot just copy the in-game number. Instead, match by eDPI or, even better, by cm/360.

  • Keep DPI identical everywhere. Since DPI is hardware, it carries across all games automatically. Leaving it at one value (like 800) removes a whole variable.
  • Match by cm/360 for the truest feel. Measure your turn distance in your main game, then adjust each new game's sensitivity until you get the same cm/360. This is the most accurate way to transfer feel.
  • Use eDPI as a quick approximation. If you do not want to measure, matching eDPI gets you close in many cases, though game-specific scaling means it is not always exact.
  • Account for FOV and aspect ratio. Different field-of-view settings can subtly change how a matched sensitivity feels, so do a quick flick test after transferring rather than assuming it is identical.

Getting sensitivity for Valorant CS2 Apex to feel the same is mostly a matter of measuring once and converting carefully. After that, your muscle memory works for you in every title instead of restarting each time you switch games.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is a good eDPI?

For most players, a balanced eDPI sits somewhere between 400 and 800, which blends flick speed with enough control for precise micro-adjustments. Precision-focused players often go lower (200–400), while movement-heavy players sometimes go higher. There is no single correct value. Use these ranges as guardrails, find a point inside them that feels controllable, and commit to it.

What DPI should I use?

800 DPI is a widely used, reliable starting point and works well with almost any sensor. The exact DPI matters less than keeping it stable. Pick one value, do all your fine-tuning through in-game sensitivity, and avoid switching DPI between sessions. A consistent DPI keeps your eDPI and cm/360 comparisons meaningful.

How do I keep the same sensitivity across games?

Keep your DPI identical in every game, then match either your cm/360 (most accurate) or your eDPI (quick approximation) when setting each game's in-game sensitivity. Measuring your full-turn distance once and converting it per game gives the truest transfer of feel. After setting it, run a short flick test to confirm it feels right.

Is lower or higher sensitivity better for aiming?

Neither is universally better. Lower sensitivity favors stable, arm-driven precision and is popular in tactical shooters, while higher sensitivity allows faster turns and suits movement-focused games. What matters is choosing a value that lets you turn comfortably and still make small corrections, then sticking with it long enough to build muscle memory.

How long before I should change my sensitivity?

Give any setting at least two weeks of consistent play before judging it. Muscle memory takes repetition to form, and a single bad session is not evidence your sensitivity is wrong. Only change after the commitment period, and even then adjust in small 10 percent steps rather than large jumps.
