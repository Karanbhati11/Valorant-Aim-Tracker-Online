Finding the best sensitivity settings for CS2 is one of the highest-leverage changes a Counter-Strike 2 player can make, and this guide is written for exactly that audience: players grinding Premier rank, returning CS:GO veterans, and newcomers who want their crosshair to land where their eyes are looking. Sensitivity is not a magic number you copy from a stream and instantly improve. It is a personal value that balances how fast you can turn against how precisely you can stop. Below we break down why pros tend to play low, how to read CS2 eDPI, what a sane CS2 DPI is, how to convert from CS:GO, and how to lock in and practice a setting that actually sticks.

Why CS2 pros favor low sensitivity

If you watch professional Counter-Strike, one pattern jumps out immediately: almost everyone plays on a relatively low sensitivity. This is not fashion, it is mechanics. CS2 rewards stillness and precise stopping far more than fast 180-degree spins, so the entire competitive scene has converged on settings that maximize control. There are three concrete reasons low sensitivity CS2 play dominates at the top level.

Precision and micro-adjustment

At lower sensitivity, every centimeter of mouse movement maps to a smaller in-game angle, so tiny hand movements produce tiny, controllable crosshair corrections. Most CS2 duels are won by a small adjustment onto a head, not a wild flick across the screen. A lower setting gives you the resolution to make those micro-adjustments cleanly instead of overshooting.

Counter-strafing accuracy

CS2 punishes shooting while moving. To fire accurately you counter-strafe, tapping the opposite key to cancel momentum so you are effectively standing still for the shot. Lower sensitivity makes that stop-and-shoot rhythm more repeatable because your aim does not jerk when your hand resets. The combination of a steady counter-strafe and a controlled sensitivity is what produces those crisp first-bullet kills you see from pros.

Spray control

Pulling down and across to fight the AK-47 or M4 recoil pattern is a physical motion of the mouse. At very high sensitivity, the compensation movements are so small that they become twitchy and inconsistent. A lower setting spreads the recoil pattern across more mouse travel, giving your hand room to learn the shape and reproduce it under pressure.

Understanding CS2 eDPI

You cannot compare two players' sensitivities by looking at the in-game slider alone, because the slider interacts with mouse DPI. The number that actually matters is eDPI, or effective DPI. It is a single value that captures your true sensitivity regardless of which mouse you use.

The formula is simple:

  • eDPI = mouse DPI × in-game sensitivity

So a player on 400 DPI with an in-game sens of 2.0 has an eDPI of 800. A player on 800 DPI with a sens of 1.0 also has an eDPI of 800, and the two will move identically in game. eDPI is the only fair way to compare settings between players, which is why every settings discussion in Counter-Strike 2 revolves around it. When someone asks what sensitivity do CS2 pros use, the honest answer is always framed in eDPI rather than a raw slider value.

A common pro eDPI range

Across the competitive scene, a very common eDPI range sits roughly between 600 and 1000, most often built on 400 DPI. That is a general observation about where the bulk of high-level players cluster, not a stat tied to any specific named individual. Plenty of pros sit a little below or above that window, but if you land inside it you are firmly in competitive territory. For most players, starting around an eDPI of 800 is a sensible middle ground you can then tune up or down by feel.

cm/360: the most intuitive measure

eDPI is great for comparison, but cm per 360 CS2 is the most intuitive way to actually feel your sensitivity. It tells you how many centimeters you must physically drag your mouse to turn a full 360 degrees in game. A lower eDPI means more centimeters per turn and more precision; a higher eDPI means fewer centimeters and a flickier, wristier feel.

Most strong CS2 players sit somewhere between roughly 35 and 55 cm per 360. That range favors arm aiming, where you anchor your wrist and move from the elbow and shoulder, which is far more consistent over a long session than pure wrist aim. If your mousepad is at least 35 to 40 cm wide, you have room to play at the lower, more precise end of the range comfortably.

What DPI should you use for CS2?

For CS2 DPI, the most popular and reliable choice is 400 or 800. These are native, true DPI steps on the vast majority of gaming mice, meaning the sensor reports cleanly without interpolation. Higher DPI values do not make you better; they simply require a lower in-game sens to reach the same eDPI. In practice 400 and 800 are safe, proven choices that the entire scene relies on.

Pick one DPI and never touch it again. Then do all of your tuning with the in-game sensitivity slider. Changing DPI mid-journey resets your muscle memory just as much as changing sensitivity, so treat DPI as a hardware setting you configure once. How to convert from CS:GO to CS2

Good news for veterans: your sensitivity carries over directly. CS2 uses the same sensitivity scale as CS:GO, so if you played at 400 DPI and 2.0 sens in CS:GO, the exact same value feels identical in Counter-Strike 2. There is no conversion math required. Your sensitivity and m_yaw values transfer one-to-one, and your eDPI and cm/360 remain unchanged.

The one thing worth re-checking is your config. If you migrated manually or started fresh, confirm that raw input is enabled and mouse acceleration is off, because a clean default config is what guarantees the CS:GO feel survives the move. If your old sensitivity served you well, keep it and spend your energy on placement and movement instead.

Raw input and mouse settings that matter

Sensitivity does not live in a vacuum. A few supporting settings determine whether your aim is consistent or subtly unpredictable. Get these right once and forget about them.

  • Raw input on: set m_rawinput to 1 so CS2 reads your mouse directly and ignores Windows pointer settings. This is essential for one-to-one tracking.
  • Mouse acceleration off: disable acceleration in game and in Windows so the same physical movement always produces the same turn. Acceleration destroys muscle memory.
  • Windows pointer speed at 6/11: the default, unscaled setting. With raw input on this is bypassed, but keeping it neutral avoids surprises.
  • Polling rate 1000 Hz: a standard, smooth report rate for modern gaming mice.
  • Disable mouse smoothing and enhance pointer precision: both add input lag or inconsistency and should be off.

With these locked in, your in-game sensitivity is the only variable left, which is exactly what you want when you are dialing in feel. Your crosshair should also be small and high-contrast so you can judge precise placement while testing different sensitivities.

How to pick and test your own CS2 sensitivity

There is no universal best value, only the best value for your hand, mousepad, and play style. Use this process to find yours instead of copying a number blindly.

  1. Start in the common range: set 400 or 800 DPI and choose an in-game sens that lands you near 800 eDPI. This is a proven starting point.
  2. Check your mousepad room: do a full 180 swing and a precise micro-adjustment. If you run out of pad on big turns, go a touch higher; if your small corrections feel jittery, go lower.
  3. Bias toward the low end: when in doubt, drop sensitivity slightly. Precision and counter-strafing improve more from going lower than higher for most players.
  4. Commit for two weeks: pick a value and stop touching it. Constant tweaking resets muscle memory and is the single biggest mistake players make.
  5. Adjust in small steps: if you must change, move by 0.1 to 0.2 in game, not in big jumps, so your hand adapts gradually.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is a good CS2 sensitivity?

A good starting point is an eDPI of roughly 800, for example 400 DPI with an in-game sensitivity of 2.0, which gives about 40 cm per 360-degree turn. Most competitive players sit between 600 and 1000 eDPI. From that starting point, adjust slightly to match your mousepad size and how precisely you can stop, then keep it stable to build muscle memory.

What DPI is best for CS2?

400 or 800 DPI are the safest and most popular choices because they are native steps on nearly every gaming mouse and report cleanly. Higher DPI does not improve your aim; it just means you need a lower in-game sensitivity to reach the same eDPI. Pick 400 or 800, then do all your tuning with the in-game slider.

Is low sensitivity better in CS2?

For most players, yes, within reason. Lower sensitivity improves precision, counter-strafing accuracy, and spray control, which is why the pro scene leans low. The catch is mousepad space: you need enough room to make full turns comfortably. Aim for the lowest sensitivity you can use while still turning 180 degrees without running off your pad.

Does my CS:GO sensitivity work in CS2?

Yes. CS2 uses the same sensitivity scale as CS:GO, so your exact value transfers one-to-one with no conversion needed. Just confirm raw input is on and mouse acceleration is off in your config, then keep the setting you already know rather than relearning a new one.

How long should I stick with one sensitivity?

Commit to a value for at least two weeks before judging it, and ideally much longer once it feels right. Muscle memory builds gradually, and constant tweaking resets that progress every time. If you must adjust, change by small increments of 0.1 to 0.2 in game so your hand adapts smoothly.
