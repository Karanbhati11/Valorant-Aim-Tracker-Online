There's no single magic sensitivity number for Valorant, but there is a smart range and a repeatable process for finding the value that fits your hand, your mousepad, and your playstyle. This guide covers eDPI, why the game rewards lower sensitivity than you might expect, and exactly how to dial in and lock a setting you can trust instead of rebuilding it every few weeks.

Valorant is a tactical shooter where the first bullet decides most duels. That single design fact shapes everything about how you should approach sensitivity. Unlike fast arena games built around constant movement, Valorant rewards players who stop, plant their feet, and tap a precise shot at head level. Your sensitivity controls how much your crosshair moves per centimeter of mouse travel, and getting it into a sane range makes that first-bullet precision dramatically easier to land consistently.

What eDPI Is and Why It Matters More Than Raw Sensitivity

Effective DPI, or eDPI, is the true measure of how sensitive your aim actually is, because it combines your mouse hardware setting with your in-game value into one comparable number.

The formula: eDPI = mouse DPI × in-game sensitivity.

A player on 800 DPI and 0.40 in-game sensitivity has an eDPI of 320. A player on 1600 DPI and 0.20 in-game sensitivity also has an eDPI of 320, and their crosshair will move almost identically for the same hand motion. This is why comparing only the in-game slider number is meaningless. Someone mentioning they play on "0.2 sens" tells you nothing until you know their DPI too. You can plug your own numbers into our eDPI Calculator to get your exact value in seconds rather than doing the math by hand.

When you switch mice or reset your config, your in-game sensitivity might feel totally different if the new mouse runs a different DPI. Tracking eDPI protects your muscle memory: write it down, and you can recreate the exact same feel on any hardware by adjusting the in-game value to match. Treat eDPI as your real setting and the two underlying numbers as just the ingredients.

Why Valorant Favors Lower eDPI

Valorant's gunplay is built around standing still and tapping. The Vandal one-taps, the Sheriff punishes, and moving accuracy is intentionally poor. In this environment, a lower eDPI gives you more mouse travel per degree of in-game rotation, meaning finer control over tiny adjustments. When an enemy head appears and your crosshair is two pixels off, a lower sensitivity lets you correct with a controlled nudge instead of an over-twitchy jump that overshoots the target.

Higher sensitivity has the opposite trade-off. It lets you whip around to clear your back quickly, but it makes precise micro-corrections harder because the smallest hand tremor translates into a big crosshair jump. Since Valorant duels are usually won by whoever plants the first accurate shot, most serious players lean toward the lower end of the spectrum.

Every sensitivity choice is really a balance between two competing needs: precision, favored by lower eDPI and best for long-range taps and micro-adjustments, and speed, favored by higher eDPI and best for checking flanks and reacting to surprise peeks. The goal isn't to maximize one and ignore the other, but to find the point where you can still flick comfortably across your pad for a 180-degree turn while keeping enough control to tap a distant head without jitter. For most players that sweet spot sits lower than instinct first suggests.

Typical eDPI Ranges at a Competitive Level

The reality is that strong competitive settings cluster within a range rather than a single fixed value, and copying any one player's exact setup won't instantly transfer their skill. That said, the common range is a useful starting point: many competitive players land somewhere around roughly 200 to 400 eDPI, most commonly built on 800 DPI. This is a typical range, not a guarantee. If your eDPI is well above this band, you're likely sacrificing first-bullet control, and dropping toward it is usually worth testing.

How to Pick and Test Your Own Sensitivity

Finding the right setting is a process, not a guess.

  1. Set your DPI to a clean value. 800 DPI is a sensible, widely used baseline that keeps the math easy and works well with most modern sensors. You can confirm your mouse is reporting a true, non-interpolated value with our DPI Analyzer.
  2. Pick a target eDPI in the common range. Start around 280 to 320 if you're unsure. Calculate the in-game value by dividing your target eDPI by your DPI, or just run it through the eDPI Calculator directly.
  3. Run the 180 test. Place your mouse on one side of your pad. A single comfortable swipe should turn you roughly 180 degrees in game. Running out of pad before turning means your sens may be too low for your space; a tiny flick spinning you fully around means it's too high.
  4. Test both extremes of aiming. Try long-range taps on distant targets and quick close-range flicks. The right sens lets you do both without feeling like you're fighting the mouse.
  5. Give it real time. Commit to a value for at least one to two weeks before judging it. Constant switching is the single biggest reason players never build stable aim.

Match sensitivity to your mousepad and grip

Your physical setup matters as much as the number itself. A small mousepad can't support a very low eDPI, since you'll run out of room mid-turn. Arm aimers who move from the elbow tend to thrive on lower sensitivities, while wrist aimers often sit a touch higher. Pick a large pad if you can, lower your sens toward the precise end, and let your arm do the work for long-range control.

Field of View and Its Interaction With Aim

Sensitivity isn't the only setting shaping how your aim feels. Field of view changes how much of the map is visible and subtly affects how far a target appears to travel across your screen for the same in-game turn, which is part of why a sensitivity that felt right at one FOV can feel slightly off after changing it. If you tweak your FOV, it's worth rechecking your setting with our FOV Calculator to see how it interacts with your current sensitivity before assuming your aim itself has gotten worse.

Scoped Sensitivity Multiplier

Valorant includes a separate scoped sensitivity multiplier that scales your aim while zoomed in with weapons like the Operator, Marshal, or the Guardian and Bulldog scopes. By default this is set to 1, keeping your scoped feel consistent with your hipfire aim relative to the zoom level. For most players, leaving it at or very near 1 is the right call, since it preserves a single consistent muscle memory across all your aiming.

If Operator flicks feel too fast or too slow compared to your rifle aim, you can nudge this value slightly, but resist tuning it heavily. The more your scoped and unscoped sensitivities diverge, the more you're asking your brain to learn two separate aiming systems, and consistency almost always beats micro-optimization here.

The Importance of Consistency

The most overlooked truth about sensitivity is that the exact number matters far less than how long you commit to it. Aim is muscle memory, and every time you change your eDPI, you partially reset the calibration your brain has built between hand motion and crosshair movement. Players who hop between settings every few days never give that memory a chance to solidify, then blame their aim when the real problem is instability.

Pick a value in a reasonable range, write down your eDPI, and stick with it. Resist the temptation to copy a new setting every time you watch a stream or have a rough night. A bad session is almost never your sensitivity's fault. Trust the process and let repetition wire the setting into your reflexes.

Practicing Your Chosen Sensitivity in a Trainer

Once you've locked an eDPI, the fastest way to build comfort with it is deliberate practice. A browser aim trainer is ideal because it lets you drill clicking, flicking, and micro-adjustments on demand without loading into a match.

  • Static precision clicks: settle your crosshair completely before each shot to mirror Valorant's stop-to-shoot gunplay and confirm your sens allows fine control.
  • Wide flicks: snap to targets at the edge of the screen and stop on them cleanly. Constantly overshooting means your eDPI may be too high; undershooting means it may be too low for your space.
  • Micro-adjustments: drill the tiny one-to-five degree corrections that turn body shots into headshots. This is where lower sensitivities shine.
  • Short tracking: follow a strafing target briefly to prepare for jiggle-peekers and wide swings.
